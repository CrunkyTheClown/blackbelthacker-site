Friday the 13th Grounded America. By Midnight, Washington Sent a $50 Billion Ransom Note.

2026-11-14 · trend, aviation, resilience, single-point-of-failure, incident-response, opinion

There is a word for fear of Friday the 13th: paraskevidekatriaphobia. Most of us in security do not have it. We have something worse: a calendar full of Friday incidents.

Yesterday gave the superstitious plenty to work with. In the afternoon, the FAA ordered a rare nationwide ground stop. Every departing flight in the United States stayed on the ground for more than four hours. Thousands of flights were canceled or delayed, Air Canada pulled its US flights for the night, and US bound aircraft sat on the ground in Mexico.

The first question everyone asked was: was it a cyberattack?

The answer was no. The second thing that happened is the one I cannot stop thinking about.

What happened

As of this morning:

  • The trigger was old telecom gear, not an attacker. The Transportation Secretary blamed the FAA’s legacy analog telecommunications network. Switches and routers became overloaded with data and started returning inconsistent information, while the newer IP switches stayed up.
  • Officials ruled out an attack. The Homeland Security secretary said there was no evidence of a cyberattack, and the FAA head said there was zero evidence of anything nefarious.
  • It started local and went national. Chicago traffic was halted over communications problems before the nationwide order came down.
  • It was not the Microsoft outage or the new AI program. Officials said it was unconnected to a separate Microsoft outage the day before, and a contractor said the newly deployed AI tool was not behind it.

The stop lifted Friday evening. Airlines spent the weekend unwinding crews and aircraft.

Then came the ask

Here is the timeline that made me sit up:

When What Washington asked Congress for
July 2025 $12.5 billion approved as a “down payment”
Late 2025 into 2026 “Another $19 to $20 billion to complete it”
September 2026 $30 billion more, one day after a Philadelphia telecom failure stopped Northeast flights
Thursday, November 12 $30.1 billion, in a formal letter to lawmakers
Friday night, November 13 $50 billion, hours after the ground stop

That is a $20 billion increase in roughly 24 hours. Reporting on the Friday night briefing says the new figure was not fully fleshed out. The Transportation Secretary’s words were simply: “I want $50 billion.”

I have spent more than two decades in incident response. I know what it looks like when someone uses a crisis to set the price.

Why this reads like a ransom note to me

Let me be clear about what I am and am not saying. Nobody broke the law here, and asking Congress for money is how government works. But the shape of this ask is the same shape I see in every extortion case I have worked: the system goes down, the clock is ticking, the pain is public, and the price arrives before the investigation does.

Here is why I am skeptical:

  1. The price came before the root cause. The FBI was still reportedly looking at whether a software update was involved. The number jumped anyway. In IR, you never agree to a payment before you have scoped the incident.
  2. The money does not match the failure. What broke on Friday was telecommunications. Yet of the $30.1 billion request from the day before, only $10.1 billion was for air traffic control modernization, while $18 billion was for airport projects, including $10 billion for terminals. Terminals did not ground a single flight on Friday.
  3. The modernization may be part of the problem. The Homeland Security secretary described a “patchwork” upgrade process that may have overwhelmed the older equipment still in use. If the transition itself is fragile, more money pushed through the same process faster is not obviously a fix.
  4. The estimates only go one direction. The telecommunications line alone has already climbed from $4.75 billion to $5.91 billion. The program’s predecessor, NextGen, is widely regarded as having delivered less than promised while costing more than planned.
  5. The timing is a pattern. September’s $30 billion ask came a day after a telecom failure. Friday’s $50 billion ask came hours after one. Every outage becomes a fundraising event.

The case for the money

To be fair, here is the strongest version of the other side, and some of it is right:

  • The system really is old. Analog switches and copper lines are still carrying safety critical traffic. Nobody disputes that.
  • Progress is real. The administration says 68 percent of the legacy copper network has already been replaced with fiber. The new IP switches stayed up on Friday while the old ones failed, which is an argument for finishing the job.
  • Underfunding has a cost too. Officials argue Congress has kicked the can for years and that each delay means more outages like this one.
  • Support is bipartisan. Lawmakers from both parties have backed additional modernization funding, and industry groups have endorsed the requests.

I agree the system needs replacing. My problem is not the goal. It is paying a number that moved $20 billion overnight, before anyone has published what actually failed.

What a defender would demand before paying

If this were a ransomware negotiation at a client, here is what I would require before a single dollar moved. Congress should ask for the same:

  1. A published root cause analysis. What failed, why the redundancy did not catch it, and what change, if any, set it off.
  2. A line item budget mapped to failure modes. Show which dollars fix the thing that broke on Friday, and which are unrelated.
  3. Milestones with consequences. Tranches released on delivery, not on press conferences.
  4. An independent assessment. The GAO or an outside team validating the estimate before the second tranche, given NextGen’s history.
  5. Resilience first. Fund the telecom backbone and the failover now. Terminals can wait for a normal budget cycle.

What defenders should take from it

The lesson for the rest of us has nothing to do with Washington:

  • Map your single points of failure, not just your vulnerabilities. One network stopped a country with no attacker involved. An attacker studying the same map could do it on purpose.
  • Treat changes to critical paths like incidents waiting to happen. Staged rollouts, a tested rollback, someone watching the blast radius. Especially on a Friday.
  • Do not let “not a cyberattack” end the investigation. Accidental outages show exactly what an adversary would target.
  • Get ahead of the phishing wave. Every mass disruption is followed by fake refund and rebooking scams. Frontier confirmed an active phishing campaign using real booking details earlier this month. Stranded travelers are next.

Briefing line for leadership

Friday’s ground stop was not an attack. The $50 billion ask that followed it should still be handled like one: scope the incident, publish the root cause, then negotiate the price.

Checklist

  • List your top five single points of failure (network, vendor, identity, update channel, DNS)
  • Confirm each has a tested failover or a documented manual mode
  • Freeze or stage changes to critical paths before weekends and holidays
  • Require a root cause analysis before approving any emergency spend
  • Pre write a customer warning for outage themed phishing
